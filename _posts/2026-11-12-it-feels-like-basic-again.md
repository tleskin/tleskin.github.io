I have this romantic thought I keep coming back to, and I don't quite know where to put it. I don't have a YouTube channel. I barely post anywhere. But it feels like the kind of thing other people would recognize the second they hear it.

Building things with AI right now — describing an app, watching it appear, fixing it, using it that same evening — feels like the stories people tell about the early days of home computers.

Not the big-company version of that history. The small, personal one.

I should be honest about that up front, though: I never programmed in BASIC, and I wasn't alive back in that day. I wasn't there for that first era. Everything I know about it is secondhand — from reading Steve Jobs's book and from hearing Steve Wozniak talk about how it was back then.

## I was around for the era after that

I was around for the early computing that came after it, though, and I remember how magical that felt.

I remember going over to my friend's house and getting on his computer — the magic of using DOS and booting up games like Commander Keen and Doom and Wolfenstein 3D. None of it was just clicking an icon and watching it load. You had to make it happen, and when the game finally came up, it felt like you'd done something.

I remember going to my aunt's school — she was a teacher — and playing on the Apple IIes they had there. And when I was in kindergarten, back in the early '90s, we had an entire lab of just Apple computers. I remember playing on those, and Kid Pix, and how fun it all was.

That whole early computing era was so fun. The computers weren't sealed boxes that did everything for you. Getting into them — even just getting a game or a drawing program running — was part of the experience.

## The romance was never the machine

When people talk about the original Apple computers and that whole first wave of home machines, what they get nostalgic about usually isn't the hardware. The hardware was slow, expensive for what it did, and kind of a pain.

What they remember is what people did with it.

Somebody would write a little program in BASIC — sometimes typed in, line by line, from a listing printed in a magazine — and if it worked, they didn't just keep it. They copied it. They brought it to a user group meeting. They handed a floppy disk to a friend and said, "try this, I made it last weekend."

The program didn't have to change the world. Most of them were tiny: a grade tracker, a recipe box, a game, a tool that renamed files or kept score or did one specific job for one specific person. The point was that a computer stopped being only something a company sold you, finished and sealed. It became something you could make do a thing you wanted — and then give that thing to someone else.

That giving-it-away part is the whole romance, I think.

## The same loop, in plain English

That's exactly the loop I'm living in now, and I think a lot of other people are too.

You have an itch. Something in your actual life is annoying and a little too specific for a big app to bother with. You describe it in plain words — not code, just words — and an hour later there's a rough version of it on your screen. You poke at it. It breaks in a funny way. You fix it. By the end of the night it's doing the job.

And then comes the part that really rhymes: you send it to someone. Not a launch. Not a product announcement. Just a link, and a message that amounts to the modern version of that floppy disk — "hey, I made this, see if it works for you."

The language changed. BASIC was the plain, learnable language that let regular people in the door back then. Plain English is the one doing that job now. You no longer have to learn the language first before you're allowed to have the idea.

## I felt it once before, with Rails

This isn't actually the first time I've had this feeling. The first time was at Turing, my code school.

You'd get an idea, and you could build the entire thing — start to finish, on your own. We were using Ruby on Rails, and Rails made that easy because it handled everything in one place: the database, the back end, the front end. You didn't need a team, and you didn't need to hand the interesting part off to somebody else. One person could hold the whole thing in their head and actually finish it.

That was the excitement I came into this work with.

Somewhere along the way, I lost a lot of it. For a while now, coding has mostly just been what I do for my job. I'd do it all day, and I didn't feel motivated to build anything on my own anymore. Not burned out, exactly — just done wanting to make things for the fun of it after work.

This has brought it back. Building with AI feels like Rails did then — an idea, and then the whole finished thing, without all the distance in between — and it has reignited my excitement about software development. It has kind of taken me full circle.

Building is fun again.

## The honest difference

I don't want to pretend it's identical, because it isn't — and the difference matters to me.

Typing in a BASIC listing taught you something whether you wanted to learn it or not. By the hundredth line, you had started to understand how the machine thought. When you skip the language entirely, you can build the thing and learn almost nothing about how it works. That can leave you stuck the first time it breaks in a way you can't describe.

I'm a working software engineer, and I still want my hands on the keyboard. I like knowing how a thing is put together. I don't want a tool that does the interesting part for me and hands me the result — I want one that gets the tedious parts out of the way so I can spend more time on the parts I actually enjoy.

So for me, the sweet spot isn't "describe it and never look at it." It's describe it, look at it, change it, understand it a little better than I did yesterday. The door is wider now. What you do once you're inside still counts.

## Small tools for real people are the ones that last

If this era follows the same pattern as the last one — and I think it will — the things people remember won't only be the huge, polished platforms. They'll be the small tools somebody made for their club, their family, their side project, their weirdly specific problem, and then shared because someone else turned out to have the same problem.

That's always been the best part of this stuff for me: the creativity of it. Designing the thing, balancing what it should and shouldn't do, making something that didn't exist that morning and handing it to a real person by nightfall. That feeling didn't start with AI, and it won't end with whatever replaces these tools. AI just lowered the price of admission back down to where it was when a kid with a magazine listing could pay it.

We are, once again, passing programs around.

They just travel by link now instead of by floppy disk.
